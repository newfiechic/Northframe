# User Case: The Cereal Box Problem

## Scenario

A company develops a cereal.

The cereal is complete. Manufacturing is established. The product works exactly as intended.

Now the company needs people to buy it.

The first response is to improve the packaging.

The box is redesigned.

The colours are changed.

The logo is modernized.

The wording is improved.

The product now looks better on the shelf, but the fundamental question remains:

**Why should someone choose this cereal instead of every other cereal beside it?**

The company has improved presentation without necessarily improving marketability.

So another solution is introduced:

**Put a free toy inside the box.**

Now there is an additional reason to purchase the cereal.

At first glance, the strategy works.

The toy creates attention. It differentiates the box. It may increase sales and give consumers an immediate incentive to choose the product.

But the company has not necessarily solved the original problem.

It has added an external acquisition mechanism to compensate for a product that was not originally designed with enough marketability built into its architecture.

## The Hidden Cost

The toy is not free to the company.

It must be manufactured or purchased.

It takes up space.

It adds weight.

It changes packaging requirements.

It may introduce safety warnings, age restrictions, compliance requirements or additional production complexity.

The company may even reduce the amount of cereal in the box to compensate for the added cost or physical space.

The customer sees:

**FREE TOY INSIDE**

The business sees:

**additional cost required to create a reason to choose the product.**

Worse, the company may now be acquiring customers who want the toy rather than customers who value the cereal.

If the toy disappears, demand created by the toy may disappear with it.

The acquisition mechanism is therefore dependent on something external to the core product.

## The Architectural Failure

The mistake was not necessarily using a toy.

Promotions, incentives, branding and advertising can all be useful.

The failure occurred earlier.

The cereal was developed first.

Its marketability was considered afterward.

The company effectively asked:

**“We have made the product. How do we convince people to buy it?”**

That question creates a very different business architecture than asking:

**“What would make this product naturally worth discovering, choosing, using, recommending and buying again?”**

The first approach builds the product and then searches for marketing.

The second designs marketability alongside the product.

## Built-In Marketability

A product with built-in marketability does not eliminate the need for marketing.

Instead, it gives marketing something meaningful to amplify.

Before development is complete, the business considers:

* Why will someone notice this?
* Why will they choose it?
* Why will they continue using or buying it?
* Why might they tell someone else about it?
* What makes the product meaningfully different?
* Where does distribution naturally occur?
* How does customer behaviour support growth?
* How does the revenue model reinforce rather than fight adoption?
* What information will customers provide that can improve the product itself?

These are not questions reserved for the marketing department after launch.

They are product architecture questions.

## The Difference

### Added Marketability

Build cereal.

Put it in a box.

Try to sell it.

Improve the box.

Still struggle.

Add a toy.

Advertise the toy.

Depend on the toy to create demand.

### Built-In Marketability

Determine why the cereal should exist.

Determine who will choose it and why.

Design the product, experience, positioning, distribution and business model around those reasons.

Then build the cereal.

Marketing now communicates an existing advantage rather than attempting to manufacture one afterward.

## Key Principle

**Marketing should amplify a reason to choose. It should not be responsible for inventing one after the product has already been built.**

A prettier cereal box may improve presentation.

A free toy may create an incentive.

Neither automatically fixes a product whose marketability was never considered as part of its original architecture.

The strongest business model does not begin with:

**“How do we market what we built?”**

It begins with:

**“What are we building that can actually be marketed, and how does that marketability exist inside the system from the beginning?”**
