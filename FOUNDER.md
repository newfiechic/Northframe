# Founder Background

## Brenda Penton

Northframe did not begin as a single business idea.

It developed from a way of looking at businesses, people, technology, marketing, and systems that has followed me for most of my life.

I have always been interested in how things work together.

Even when I was young, I was fascinated by businesses beyond the products they sold. I watched how companies presented themselves, how people responded to marketing, why certain ideas attracted attention while others did not, how reputation affected decisions, and how businesses changed as they grew.

I was particularly interested in patterns.

I liked watching something evolve over time and trying to understand why it evolved the way it did. I naturally looked for relationships between decisions, behaviour, outcomes, and the larger structure surrounding them.

Long before I knew terms such as systems thinking, business architecture, customer psychology, user behaviour, or pattern mapping, I was already approaching problems from that perspective.

Rather than seeing individual pieces as isolated, I tended to ask how they connected.

Why did this work?

Why did that fail?

What changed?

What was overlooked?

What happens further down the line because of a decision being made here?

That curiosity eventually became the foundation of the way I approach business today.

## The Limits of Crypto Marketing

Crypto marketing has developed its own highly specialized culture.

Certain formulas are repeated because they work.

Projects commonly rely on:

- community-building
- influencers
- social media visibility
- narratives
- partnerships
- trending services
- launch events
- token incentives
- competitions
- engagement campaigns
- reputation
- hype cycles
- community raids
- platform-specific promotional strategies

These approaches can be effective.

However, I became increasingly interested in what happens when marketing reaches the limits of the business underneath it.

A strong campaign cannot permanently compensate for weak product design.

Attention cannot automatically create useful differentiation.

Community cannot solve every revenue problem.

Reputation cannot be the only acquisition mechanism.

Marketing cannot manufacture meaningful utility indefinitely if that utility was never designed into the project.

Eventually I became less interested in asking:

**How do we market this?**

and more interested in asking:

**What needed to exist before marketing ever reached this point?**

That question changed the direction of my work.

## Moving Upstream

I began looking further upstream.

If marketing was struggling, I wanted to know whether the problem actually originated in marketing.

Sometimes it did.

Often it did not.

A marketing problem could originate in positioning.

A positioning problem could originate in product design.

A customer acquisition problem could originate in the absence of differentiation.

A retention problem could originate in utility.

A revenue problem could originate in the original business model.

A development problem could continue because information learned from users or marketing had no route back into development.

A project could rely heavily on reputation simply because no independent acquisition system had ever been designed.

At that point, optimizing the downstream function does not necessarily solve the upstream structural problem.

This became one of the central ideas behind Northframe.

## Pattern Mapping

Pattern recognition has always been one of the strongest threads connecting my interests.

I tend to remember relationships between events, decisions, behaviours, and outcomes.

When examining a business, I naturally begin mapping those relationships.

If something changes here, what changes there?

If this department learns something, who else needs that information?

If this feature is removed, what does marketing lose?

If marketing attracts a certain type of customer, what does development learn?

If revenue depends on this behaviour, what happens if the behaviour disappears?

If a business depends on reputation, what happens before reputation exists?

If a company grows, which assumptions that worked at a smaller scale stop working?

I am interested in the hidden dependencies.

They are often where both risk and opportunity exist.

## Psychology and Business

Another recurring interest throughout my life has been psychology.

Businesses are human systems.

Products are created by people.

Companies are operated by people.

Marketing communicates with people.

Customers interpret information through perception, expectation, emotion, experience, trust, identity, motivation, and existing assumptions.

Because of that, I have never found it useful to separate business completely from human behaviour.

Marketing made that especially obvious.

A technically accurate message is not necessarily persuasive.

A useful product is not automatically perceived as useful.

Attention is not the same as trust.

Trust is not the same as conversion.

Conversion is not the same as retention.

Customer behaviour cannot be understood entirely by examining the product.

This interest in psychology became another part of the way I examined business structures.

## Top-Down Rather Than Bottom-Up

One of the strongest ideas emerging from my work is the distinction between approaching a business problem from the bottom up and approaching it from the top down.

A bottom-up approach usually begins where the problem becomes visible.

Marketing is weak, so improve marketing.

Sales are weak, so improve sales.

Retention is weak, so improve retention.

Development is slow, so improve development.

Those interventions may be useful.

But they assume the visible problem originated in the same place where it appeared.

A top-down approach begins with the larger system.

What is the business trying to accomplish?

What needs to exist for that outcome to become possible?

What functions depend on one another?

What information needs to move between them?

What was designed upstream?

What was omitted?

What assumptions does the current structure depend on?

Only then do we move downstream.

Northframe grew from my interest in making that process explicit.

---

## Built-In Marketability

Marketing became one of the clearest examples.

Traditionally, a product can be built and marketing can be asked afterward to promote it.

But that creates an obvious limitation.

Marketing can only work with what development and the larger business have already created.

If nothing within the product generates stories, differentiation, visual identity, user participation, useful data, customer outcomes, community interaction, or reasons to return, marketing has much less material available.

This led me toward the idea of **built-in marketability**.

Rather than asking how to market a finished product, the question becomes:

**What could be designed into the product or business so that marketability exists before promotion begins?**

That changes marketing from a downstream promotional department into an input during business and product design.

It also creates a feedback loop.

Marketing learns what users respond to.

Users reveal needs and friction.

That information returns to development.

Development changes the product.

Those changes create new marketing opportunities.

The system becomes iterative rather than linear.

## Northframe

Northframe is the framework I am developing to formalize this way of thinking.

It examines businesses as interconnected systems rather than collections of isolated departments.

The process begins with the intended outcome and works downward through the structures required to support it.

It looks for dependencies.

It looks for missing relationships.

It looks at where information stops moving.

It examines where downstream problems may have been created by upstream decisions.

It considers psychology alongside technology and operations.

It treats marketing not merely as promotion but as part of the architecture of the business itself.

And it asks a question that has followed me throughout most of my life:

**How do all of these pieces affect one another?**

## Northframe as a Developing Framework

Northframe is intentionally being developed as an ongoing process rather than presented as a finished doctrine.

I want the framework to evolve through application.

That means documenting cases.

Testing assumptions.

Finding exceptions.

Identifying where an idea does not work.

Comparing different business structures.

Watching how projects evolve.

Looking for recurring patterns.

Adding concepts when they prove useful.

Removing them when they do not.

The repository itself therefore forms part of the development process.

It documents not simply a finished answer, but the evolution of the framework.
